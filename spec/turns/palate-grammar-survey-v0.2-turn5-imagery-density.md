# Palate Grammar Survey v0.2 — Turn 5: Imagery & Density Grammars

This document covers two of the four remaining axes: **Imagery** and **Density**. Both are written with five-bucket altname blocks from the start, in the authentic-voice discipline established in Turn 4 (revised). All vocabulary is grounded in published community sources — Lovable's docs, Vercel's v0 prompting guide, awesome-claude-design, the AI Slop catalogs (925Studios, Monet), Material Design density specs, and working-designer commentary on stock photography and information density.

The routing-test discipline applies: a substyle earns its place only if a brief can route to it without routing to its parent grammar's other substyles.

---

# AXIS 6 — IMAGERY GRAMMAR

Imagery grammar is the system of decisions about: what photographic, illustrative, or rendered visual content lives on the page, how it's composed, what it's of, and how it interacts with type and layout. Imagery is the axis where AI-generated sites are *most visible* in their failures — generic stock photos of "diverse team huddled around laptop," default Unsplash hero images, AI-generated illustrations with telltale soft shadows and uncanny anatomy. Imagery decisions are also where a site's brand register is most palpably set — a black-and-white documentary photograph and a 3D-rendered abstract object signal entirely different products.

Imagery grammar is distinct from layout (where images sit) and from color (which palette dominates them). A vertical-rhythm-editorial layout can host any of several imagery grammars depending on register.

The routing tests here are stricter than for other axes because imagery overlaps significantly with content (what photographs *show*) which a design system can only partially specify. The grammars below name the *imagery treatment* — the visual register, sourcing approach, and composition discipline — not the literal subject matter.

There are nine imagery grammars worth distinguishing.

---

## IMG-1. Editorial Photography (Story-Driven Visual)

**Definition.** Photography that operates like a magazine spread — composed, considered, often featuring people or places in narrative context. The image carries information beyond decoration; it's *editorial*, meaning it does the work of helping the reader understand a story. Lighting is intentional (often natural), composition uses negative space deliberately, and photographs are usually full-bleed or large-format.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Lifestyle Photography* — Editorial is composed and intentional; Lifestyle is candid-coded and warmer. Versus *Documentary Photography* — Editorial is art-directed; Documentary captures unvarnished moments. Versus *Product Photography* — Editorial places products in lived contexts; Product Photography isolates them on neutral grounds.

**Substyles.**
- **Travel and Place Editorial.** Photography of locations — landscapes, interiors, architecture — composed to evoke a sense of place. Hospitality, travel publications, considered consumer brands.
- **Fashion Editorial.** Composed photography of people in clothing or with products, often with high-contrast lighting and stylized poses. Fashion houses, beauty brands, editorial publications.
- **People Editorial.** Portraits and lived-environment photography of people. Often in natural light, often communicating something about character or context. Brand sites that want to feel human-led.
- **Object Editorial.** Composed photography of objects — food, products, artifacts — treated as subjects worthy of editorial framing rather than as items for sale. Premium product brands, food publications, considered consumer goods.

**Canonical examples.** Aman properties, Soho House, Apartamento, Cereal Magazine, Hermès editorial, Loewe, NYT Style, considered fashion houses, premium hospitality, food publications like Cherry Bombe.

**Internal logic.**
- Photographs are *large* — full-bleed alternation with text, or major composition anchors.
- Subjects are framed with negative space; photographs are not "filled to the edges."
- Color treatment is restrained — natural light, often slightly muted, occasionally graded toward warmer or cooler tones depending on brand register.
- Text appears alongside, not on top of, images. Overlay text is minimal.
- Stock photography is forbidden — editorial photography is custom or commissioned.

**Registers it hosts.** Luxury hospitality, considered fashion, editorial publishing, premium food and beverage, fine arts, refined consumer brands, brands where "made by people who care" is the brand statement.

**Registers it resists.** SaaS marketing, B2B, application UIs, anywhere imagery is decorative rather than narrative. Anywhere stock photography is the only economically viable option.

**Failure mode.** AI conflates editorial photography with stock photography that *looks* editorial — the hero image of a woman holding a coffee cup looking thoughtfully out a window. The composition resembles editorial photography but the image is generic, used by hundreds of other brands. Real editorial photography is custom.

- **Vibes:** *editorial* — *magazine-style photography* — *art-directed* — *like a real publication* — *premium photography* — *considered* — *intentional* — *full-bleed photography*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Aman's photography* — *like Apartamento* — *like Cereal Magazine* — *like a Soho House feature* — *like Hermès editorial* — *like NYT Style*
- **Vernacular labels:** *editorial photography* — *magazine photography* — *full-bleed photography* — *story-driven imagery* — *photography-led design*
- **Anti-vibes:** *no stock photos* — *no "diverse team huddled around laptop"* — *no smiling office workers* — *no corporate handshakes* — *not Unsplash-tier*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want photography that does real work, not decoration* — *I need images that signal "real publication"* — *the photography should feel custom, not stock* — *I want it to feel like a magazine, not a sales page*

## IMG-2. Lifestyle Photography (Aspirational-Authentic)

**Definition.** Photography of people doing things, in places, using products — composed to feel candid and aspirational simultaneously. The line between editorial and lifestyle is whether the photography is doing narrative work (editorial) or atmospheric work (lifestyle). Lifestyle photography sells a feeling of how it feels to be the kind of person who uses the product.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Editorial Photography* — Lifestyle is warmer and atmosphere-driven; Editorial is composed and story-driven. Versus *Documentary Photography* — Lifestyle is aspirational and lightly art-directed; Documentary is unvarnished. Versus *Stock Photography* — Lifestyle is custom-shot or carefully curated; Stock is interchangeable.

**Substyles.**
- **Sun-Soaked Lifestyle.** Warm, golden-hour photography of people in inviting environments — beaches, kitchens, gardens. Travel, food, wellness, beauty brands.
- **Quiet-Moment Lifestyle.** Slower, contemplative photography of solo subjects or intimate moments. Wellness brands, premium consumer goods, considered lifestyle.
- **Active Lifestyle.** Photography of people in motion — running, working, creating. Fitness, athletic brands, productivity products.
- **Domestic Lifestyle.** Photography of homes, kitchens, families. Home goods, family-oriented brands, considered domestic products.

**Canonical examples.** Glossier, Aesop, Airbnb (the warm-coral marketing photography), Patagonia, considered DTC consumer brands, Headspace, Mejuri, premium athletic brands.

**Internal logic.**
- Photography is warm — color graded toward warmth, often shot in golden hour or with soft natural light.
- Subjects are people in *real-feeling* contexts, not posed studio-coded contexts.
- Photographs are large but typically not as full-bleed as editorial — more often arranged in galleries or beside text.
- The atmosphere is the message; the specific detail is secondary.
- Custom photography is strongly preferred; carefully curated lifestyle stock is acceptable in a way it isn't for editorial.

**Registers it hosts.** Beauty, wellness, lifestyle DTC brands, food and beverage, athletic and outdoor brands, home goods, considered consumer products.

**Registers it resists.** B2B, enterprise, application UIs, anything technical, hospitality at the top tier (which prefers Editorial Photography). News and journalism (which prefers Documentary).

**Failure mode.** AI substitutes generic lifestyle stock — the laptop on a wooden table with coffee, the woman jogging in a park — and the result reads as "could be any DTC brand." Lifestyle photography requires *brand-specific* atmosphere; generic warmth is the failure mode.

- **Vibes:** *warm and inviting* — *aspirational* — *lived-in* — *golden-hour* — *sun-soaked* — *that DTC brand feel* — *Glossier-coded* — *Aesop-warm*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Glossier's photography* — *like Aesop* — *like Airbnb's marketing photos* — *like Patagonia* — *like Mejuri*
- **Vernacular labels:** *lifestyle photography* — *aspirational photography* — *DTC brand photography* — *warm photography* — *brand photography*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not stock-photo-coded* — *no fake-candid laptops on tables* — *not corporate* — *not studio-coded poses*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want photography that sells a feeling, not a product* — *I need it to feel like real life, but better* — *make it warm and inviting* — *I want users to imagine themselves here*

## IMG-3. Documentary Photography (Unvarnished-Real)

**Definition.** Photography that prioritizes truth over composition — captured moments rather than composed ones, often with apparent imperfection (motion blur, mid-action, available light only). The photography signals authenticity by refusing the aesthetic perfection of editorial or the warmth of lifestyle.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Editorial* — Documentary refuses art direction; Editorial embraces it. Versus *Lifestyle* — Documentary refuses warmth and aspiration; Lifestyle is built on it. Versus *Behind-the-Scenes* (a substyle here) — Documentary captures external subjects; BTS captures the brand's own work.

**Substyles.**
- **Photojournalism.** News-coded photography of events, people, situations. Journalism, cause-driven brands, advocacy organizations.
- **Behind-the-Scenes Documentary.** Photography of the brand's own work being made — workshops, production, process. Heritage brands, makers, craft-coded products.
- **Street Photography.** Urban candid photography of people and places. Lifestyle brands wanting urban credibility, urban-coded fashion.
- **Reportage Documentary.** Long-form photographic essays on subjects — communities, places, issues. Journalism-coded brands, cause-driven publishing.

**Canonical examples.** NYT, Magnum-style brand work, Patagonia's environmental campaigns, heritage menswear brands showing workshops, Christian Aid and similar cause organizations, journalism sites.

**Internal logic.**
- Photographs accept imperfection — motion blur, available light, mid-action framing all signal authenticity.
- Black-and-white is common (signals documentary register); color when used is naturalistic, not graded.
- Subjects are not posed; if they're aware of the camera, they're not performing for it.
- Captions or context matter — documentary photography usually carries information.

**Registers it hosts.** Journalism, cause-driven organizations, heritage brands wanting craft credibility, makers, considered menswear (especially heritage), urban-coded fashion, advocacy.

**Registers it resists.** Beauty (where lifestyle dominates), SaaS marketing, consumer tech, anywhere "polished and aspirational" is the brand statement.

**Failure mode.** AI rarely produces documentary photography — it requires actual cameras, actual subjects, actual moments. AI-generated "documentary-style" images are the easiest to spot (uncanny lighting, impossible compositions, the AI tell of "everyone slightly too perfect"). The grammar's authenticity discipline is what AI cannot fake.

- **Vibes:** *documentary* — *unvarnished* — *real* — *authentic* — *photojournalism* — *behind-the-scenes* — *raw* — *not staged*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Magnum's brand work* — *like Patagonia's environmental campaigns* — *like a heritage menswear workshop feature* — *journalism-coded photography*
- **Vernacular labels:** *documentary photography* — *photojournalism* — *behind-the-scenes* — *reportage* — *street photography* — *available light photography*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not staged* — *not glossy* — *no studio lighting* — *not retouched* — *no AI-generated images*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want it to feel real, not produced* — *I'm signaling authenticity through imperfection* — *the photography should look like it captured something true* — *make it feel like journalism, not advertising*

## IMG-4. Product Photography (Studio-Isolated)

**Definition.** Photography of products on neutral backgrounds, usually white or grey, often with controlled studio lighting. The product is the entire subject; environment is removed. The grammar of e-commerce, catalog sites, and product-detail surfaces.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Editorial Object Photography* — Product Photography isolates products on neutral grounds; Editorial Object treats objects as compositional subjects in lived contexts. Versus *Lifestyle Product* (a substyle) — pure Product Photography refuses environmental context; Lifestyle Product places products in environments.

**Substyles.**
- **White-Background Product.** Products isolated on pure white. The e-commerce default. Amazon, most catalog sites, most online stores.
- **Studio-Lit Product.** Products on neutral backgrounds with deliberate studio lighting that creates dimension. Premium product sites, considered consumer brands.
- **Lifestyle Product.** Products photographed in environmental contexts — a watch on a wrist, a chair in a living room. Premium consumer brands.
- **Floating Product.** Products composed with shadow as if floating, often with creative angle. Beauty, jewelry, considered product brands.
- **Top-Down Product.** Photography from directly above, often with multiple products composed in flat lay. Food, fashion, considered product brands.

**Canonical examples.** Amazon (white-background), Apple's product detail pages (studio-lit), Bang & Olufsen (lifestyle product), Glossier's product photography (floating), considered DTC brands generally.

**Internal logic.**
- Lighting is controlled — even white-background photography requires careful lighting to render product accurately.
- Products are *clean* — no clutter, no extraneous elements competing for attention.
- Color reproduction matters — products must look exactly as they do in real life.
- Often paired with Catalog layout, Soft-Container components.

**Registers it hosts.** E-commerce of all kinds, catalog sites, product detail pages, consumer products, fashion, beauty, jewelry, electronics, food packaging.

**Registers it resists.** Editorial publishing, B2B SaaS marketing (where there's no physical product to photograph), application UIs, services-based businesses without products to show.

**Failure mode.** AI produces "product photography" by generating an image of a product that *isn't real* — the result is uncanny because the product itself doesn't exist outside the image. For real products, AI often miscolors or mis-textures the product, breaking trust. Real product photography requires real products.

- **Vibes:** *product shots* — *studio photography* — *clean product images* — *e-commerce-coded* — *catalog-style* — *top-down flat lay*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Apple's product photography* — *like Bang & Olufsen* — *like Glossier's product shots* — *like an Amazon listing* — *standard e-commerce*
- **Vernacular labels:** *product photography* — *white-background photography* — *studio product photography* — *e-commerce photography* — *flat lay* — *floating product*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not editorial* — *not lifestyle* — *not generated* — *not AI-rendered products*
- **Compositional intent:** *I'm selling actual products, users need to see them clearly* — *the photography should be honest about what the product is* — *I need it to convert, not seduce*

## IMG-5. 3D Render / CGI

**Definition.** Three-dimensional rendered imagery — created in software like Cinema 4D, Blender, Spline, KeyShot — used as hero imagery, product visualization, or abstract decoration. The grammar of contemporary tech marketing, premium hardware launches, and brands signaling computational sophistication.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Product Photography* — 3D Render creates products that don't exist or shows products in impossible angles; Product Photography captures real products. Versus *Illustration* — 3D Render attempts dimensionality and (often) photorealism; Illustration embraces flatness or stylization. Versus *AI-Generated Imagery* — 3D Render is composed by humans using 3D software; AI imagery is generated by diffusion models from prompts.

**Substyles.**
- **Photorealistic 3D Render.** Renders that aim to look like photographs — premium hardware, industrial products, architectural visualization.
- **Stylized 3D Render.** Renders with deliberate non-photorealistic aesthetic — soft surfaces, bright colors, "toy faces" style, abstract objects. Common in modern AI startup marketing and contemporary tech branding.
- **Abstract 3D Render.** Non-representational 3D — geometric shapes, organic forms, flowing materials. Common as hero decoration in modern tech marketing.
- **Spline-Coded 3D.** Web-native interactive 3D using Spline or similar tools — often allowing user interaction. Modern design portfolios, premium product launches.
- **Architectural 3D Render.** Photorealistic renders of buildings, interiors, spaces. Real estate, architecture, hospitality preview imagery.

**Canonical examples.** Apple's product detail pages (photorealistic 3D), Stripe's announcement pages (abstract 3D), Linear marketing (stylized abstract), modern AI startup launches, premium hardware sites, architectural visualization studios.

**Internal logic.**
- Lighting is controlled and often dramatic — soft studio lighting, ambient occlusion, careful shadow rendering.
- Surfaces are deliberate — matte vs glossy, smooth vs textured, all signaling brand register.
- The 3D is *intentional* — the brand is signaling that it has the resources and sophistication to commission custom 3D, or that it's tech-coded enough that 3D is the natural register.
- Often paired with Marketing-Bento layout, Iridescent or Dark-Mode color, Soft-Container or Glass components.

**Registers it hosts.** Premium hardware, modern tech and AI marketing, architectural and real estate visualization, contemporary consumer tech, product launches that want to feel "produced."

**Registers it resists.** Hospitality (3D reads as tech-coded, not luxury). Editorial publishing. Heritage. Anything where "real photographs of real things" is the brand statement.

**Failure mode.** AI conflates 3D render with AI-generated imagery — they're different in technique and signal. 3D rendering is composed by humans in 3D software with full control over every surface; AI-generated imagery is sampled from training data with limited control. AI sometimes generates "3D-looking" imagery that has the visual register of CGI but the uncanny inconsistencies of diffusion models. The result reads as fake-3D, not 3D.

- **Vibes:** *3D render* — *CGI* — *Spline-coded* — *abstract 3D objects* — *photorealistic render* — *toy faces style* — *premium tech imagery*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Apple's product 3D* — *like Stripe's abstract 3D* — *like Linear's stylized 3D* — *like a modern AI startup launch* — *like a Spline portfolio*
- **Vernacular labels:** *3D render* — *CGI* — *3D illustration* — *Spline graphics* — *photorealistic render* — *abstract 3D* — *3D hero*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not photography* — *not flat illustration* — *not AI-generated images*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want to signal computational sophistication* — *I need imagery that doesn't exist in the real world* — *make it look produced, like Apple* — *I want the brand to feel premium-digital*

## IMG-6. Custom Illustration (Branded Visual Voice)

**Definition.** Hand-drawn or designer-illustrated imagery used for hero visuals, spot illustrations, mascots, decorative elements. The grammar of brands wanting *visual personality* through custom artwork rather than photography.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *3D Render* — Illustration is deliberately 2D and stylized; 3D Render attempts dimensionality. Versus *Photography* — Illustration acknowledges its made-by-hand nature; Photography presents itself as captured reality. Versus *AI-Generated Illustration* (a critical distinction) — Custom Illustration is made by human illustrators; AI-Generated Illustration is sampled from training data and has telltale aesthetic markers.

**Substyles.**
- **Flat Illustration.** Two-dimensional with limited shading, often with strong color and clean line work. Common in modern SaaS marketing, fintech, friendly tech brands.
- **Hand-Drawn Illustration.** Visibly hand-drawn — pencil, ink, watercolor textures. Lifestyle brands, food brands, considered consumer products, children's products.
- **Editorial Illustration.** Illustration that does narrative work — accompanying articles, supporting concepts, often with a single illustrator's distinctive style. Editorial publications, considered blogs, news organizations.
- **Mascot and Character Illustration.** Branded characters — mascots, recurring illustrated figures. Consumer brands wanting recognizable personality (Mailchimp's Freddie, Slack's character illustrations, Duolingo's owl).
- **Geometric and Abstract Illustration.** Non-figurative illustration — patterns, geometric shapes, abstract decorations. Modern brands wanting decoration without representation.

**Canonical examples.** Mailchimp (mascot and editorial illustration), Slack (character-driven illustration), Headspace (custom illustration throughout), considered editorial publications, fintech with friendly illustration, modern children's brands.

**Internal logic.**
- The illustration has a *consistent style* across uses — one illustrator or one style guide governs the entire system.
- Custom illustration takes time and money — its presence signals the brand has invested in distinctive visual voice.
- Often paired with Maximalist-Decorative components, Pastel-Vibrant or Earth-Pulled color, Pill-and-Cushion components.
- AI-generated illustration is a distinct grammar (see IMG-9) and should not be conflated with Custom Illustration.

**Registers it hosts.** Friendly tech brands, fintech (especially consumer-facing), wellness, lifestyle, children's products, editorial publishing with illustrated features, mid-market consumer brands.

**Registers it resists.** Luxury hospitality. Editorial high-fashion. Heritage. Anywhere photography's authenticity is the brand statement. Most B2B (where illustration can read as too-friendly).

**Failure mode.** AI generates illustration that has the *aesthetic* of custom illustration but lacks consistency across uses — every illustration looks slightly different in style because the model produces variation. Real custom illustration is *consistent*; AI-generated illustration looks like five different illustrators' work stitched together.

- **Vibes:** *custom illustration* — *hand-drawn* — *flat illustration* — *editorial illustration* — *mascot-style* — *character illustration* — *quirky illustration*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Mailchimp's illustrations* — *like Slack's characters* — *like Headspace's illustration* — *like Duolingo's owl* — *like a modern fintech brand*
- **Vernacular labels:** *custom illustration* — *flat illustration* — *hand-drawn illustration* — *editorial illustration* — *mascot illustration* — *character design*
- **Anti-vibes:** *no AI-generated illustrations* — *no stock illustrations* — *not photography* — *no inconsistent style*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want a distinctive visual voice* — *I'm building a brand with personality* — *make it feel hand-made, not generated* — *I need illustration that's recognizably ours*

## IMG-7. Stock Photography (The AI-Default Failure Mode)

**Definition.** Pre-existing licensed photography used as imagery on websites — sourced from Shutterstock, iStock, Unsplash, Pexels, Adobe Stock. The grammar of constrained-budget marketing, agency-built sites, and AI-generated sites that need imagery without commissioning custom work.

**Distinguishing edge.** Stock Photography is technically a *sourcing* decision rather than a visual register, but it functions as a grammar because the visual register of stock is recognizable: corporate-coded, generic, often featuring conventionally diverse-looking subjects in clean studio environments. The "stock photo look" is a real aesthetic, not just an absence of custom photography.

**Substyles.**
- **Shutterstock-Tier Stock.** The classic stock register — diverse team meeting in modern office, smiling customer service rep, handshake over conference table. The most generic register.
- **Unsplash-Tier Stock.** More aesthetically curated stock — natural light, considered composition, typically sourced from contributors who shoot lifestyle. Less generic than Shutterstock but still recognizable.
- **Premium Curated Stock.** Carefully selected stock from premium providers (Stocksy, Death to Stock, Twenty20) that approaches custom-photography quality.
- **Editorial Stock.** Stock photography licensed for editorial use — historical photography, archive imagery, news photography. Treated separately because editorial license differs from commercial.

**Canonical examples.** The vast majority of small-business websites, most agency-built sites, most Webflow templates, most Wix and Squarespace defaults, most AI-generated marketing pages, most generic SaaS landing pages.

**Internal logic.**
- The hallmark of stock photography is *interchangeability* — the same image can be used by a project management tool, a CRM, and a financial advisor, and none of them stand out.
- The "stock photo look" includes: studio-coded lighting, conventionally photogenic models, multiple subjects looking at a laptop or document, handshakes, smiling at the camera, "diverse team huddling" composition.
- Stock photography frequently fails to align with brand register — generic warm lifestyle stock used for fintech, generic corporate stock used for creative agencies.
- Naming this as a grammar (rather than just "absence of custom photography") helps Palate route briefs *away* from it when other grammars would be better.

**Registers it hosts.** Realistically: small-budget marketing, internal tools, B2B with no photographic resources, agency-built MVPs. Stock isn't *wrong* in every context — it's wrong when it conflicts with brand intent.

**Registers it resists.** Premium hospitality, considered fashion, editorial publishing, brands where authenticity is the brand statement. In short: most Palate-relevant briefs.

**Failure mode.** This *is* the failure mode for many briefs — vibe coders use stock photography because it's the easiest path, and the result reads as generic. The mitigation isn't always "use custom photography" (which costs money) but "choose imagery that doesn't have the stock-photo look" (which is curation work).

- **Vibes:** *stock photo look* — *Shutterstock-tier* — *generic photography* — *corporate-coded photos* — *that "diverse team" photo*
- **Brand exemplars:** *typical Shutterstock* — *Unsplash hero photo* — *generic agency-built site*
- **Vernacular labels:** *stock photography* — *stock photos* — *Unsplash photos* — *Shutterstock-tier* — *licensed photography*
- **Anti-vibes:** *no corporate handshakes* — *no "diverse team huddled around laptop"* — *no smiling office workers* — *no Unsplash-tier hero* — *not interchangeable*
- **Compositional intent:** *(this grammar's compositional intent is rarely the goal — usually the failure mode)* — *I have no budget for custom photography* — *I just need a hero image that's not embarrassing* — *use the most curated stock I can get*

## IMG-8. Iconography and System Imagery

**Definition.** The grammar of icons, glyphs, pictograms, and small system illustrations used across UI surfaces. Distinct from custom illustration because iconography is *systematic* — meant to be a coherent set used consistently across an interface.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Custom Illustration* — Iconography is systematic and used at small sizes for UI; Illustration is decorative or narrative at larger sizes. Versus *Photography* — Iconography is graphical and abstract; Photography is representational.

**Substyles.**
- **Lucide / Heroicons / Phosphor.** The major open-source icon libraries used by virtually every AI-generated and modern SaaS product. The AI default, called out explicitly in AI Slop critiques.
- **Custom Icon System.** Bespoke icons designed for a specific brand, used consistently across the product. Premium SaaS, considered design systems.
- **Material Symbols / SF Symbols.** Platform-aligned icon systems from Google and Apple, used in Material- or HIG-aligned products.
- **Decorative Icon System.** Stylized icons that are part of the brand visual voice — often with character, color, or distinctive treatment. Considered consumer brands.

**Canonical examples.** Lucide and Heroicons are the AI default for v0/Lovable/Cursor outputs. Custom icon systems exist at Stripe, Linear, Notion, Apple, IBM (Carbon), GitHub. Material Symbols at Google products, SF Symbols at Apple products.

**Internal logic.**
- Icon weight, style, and corner-treatment must be *consistent* across the system.
- Icons are *small* — typically 16–24px in interface contexts. They serve recognition, not decoration.
- Custom icon systems take real design effort; Lucide-default is the AI tell.
- Icon meaning matters — universal symbols (gear for settings, magnifying glass for search) work; novel icons require labels.

**Registers it hosts.** Every product UI. Every modern SaaS marketing site. Every application that has interface elements.

**Registers it resists.** Editorial publishing (which uses iconography sparingly if at all). Hospitality (which often uses no icons in marketing surfaces). Pure photography-led brands.

**Failure mode.** AI defaults to Lucide for everything, regardless of whether the brand would benefit from a custom icon system. Lucide's default presence is one of the AI Slop tells. The grammar exists partly to flag this so briefs can route to *Custom Icon System* when register demands it.

- **Vibes:** *icons* — *icon system* — *system imagery* — *interface icons*
- **Brand exemplars:** *Lucide icons* — *Heroicons* — *Phosphor icons* — *Material Symbols* — *Stripe's icons* — *Linear's icons*
- **Vernacular labels:** *icons* — *icon library* — *icon system* — *iconography* — *pictograms* — *glyphs*
- **Anti-vibes:** *no Hero Icons because every tutorial uses them* — *no Lucide everywhere* — *no default shadcn icons*
- **Compositional intent:** *I need a coherent icon system for the UI* — *I don't want to look like every other product using Lucide* — *I'm building a brand and the icons should match it*

## IMG-9. AI-Generated Imagery (As a Deliberate Choice)

**Definition.** Imagery generated by diffusion models — Midjourney, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, Flux, Ideogram, Adobe Firefly. Distinguished from custom illustration (made by human illustrators) and 3D render (composed by humans in 3D software) by its sourcing technique. Used deliberately as a brand choice rather than as a stand-in for custom imagery.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Custom Illustration* — AI-Generated is sampled from training data with limited stylistic control; Custom Illustration has consistent style across uses. Versus *Stock Photography* — AI-Generated is novel for each use; Stock is interchangeable. Versus *Photography* — AI-Generated has telltale aesthetic markers (uncanny anatomy, soft over-shadowing, "AI faces").

**Substyles.**
- **AI-Hero Imagery.** AI-generated hero images, often deliberately stylized to embrace the AI aesthetic rather than hide it. Modern AI products often use this self-referentially.
- **AI-Generated Illustration.** Diffusion-model output used as decorative illustration. Common in low-budget marketing where custom illustration is too expensive.
- **AI Photo-Style Imagery.** AI-generated images that try to look like photography. Highest failure rate — uncanny humans, impossible compositions, telltale AI tells.
- **AI Background and Texture.** AI-generated abstract backgrounds, textures, and decorative elements. Lower failure rate because abstract content has no "uncanny" reference.

**Canonical examples.** Modern AI product launches that use AI imagery self-referentially (OpenAI, Anthropic occasionally, AI tool startups), low-budget marketing that uses AI imagery as stock alternative, indie product launches with AI-generated hero imagery.

**Internal logic.**
- AI imagery has *recognizable tells* — soft over-rendering, anatomical inconsistencies, "everyone slightly too perfect," uncanny lighting, fabricated text in images.
- Used self-referentially (AI products with AI imagery), it can work as brand statement.
- Used as stand-in for photography or custom illustration, it usually reads as low-effort or actively wrong.
- The grammar is included partly so briefs can explicitly *opt out* of it ("no AI-generated images") and so briefs that explicitly want it can route correctly.

**Registers it hosts.** AI products signaling "we use the technology we sell." Indie launches with no budget for custom imagery. Experimental brands using AI imagery as creative direction.

**Registers it resists.** Hospitality (AI imagery breaks luxury trust). Editorial publishing (where custom photography/illustration is expected). Heritage. Anywhere authenticity matters.

**Failure mode.** This is *itself* the failure mode for most briefs — AI defaults to AI-generated imagery when it has no other option, and the result reads as low-effort. Naming this grammar lets Palate explicitly route *away* from it for briefs where it's wrong, and *toward* it for briefs (rare) where it's deliberate.

- **Vibes:** *AI-generated imagery* — *Midjourney-style* — *DALL-E images* — *diffusion-model output*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like an AI startup using AI imagery* — *like an indie launch with Midjourney heroes*
- **Vernacular labels:** *AI imagery* — *AI-generated images* — *Midjourney art* — *DALL-E generations* — *Stable Diffusion output* — *AI illustration*
- **Anti-vibes:** *no AI faces* — *no uncanny AI people* — *not AI-generated illustration* — *no Midjourney slop* — *no AI photo-style imagery* — *no AI-generated imagery at all*
- **Compositional intent:** *I'm using AI imagery deliberately as part of the brand statement* — *I'm building an AI product and the imagery should reflect that* — *(or, more often:) I explicitly DON'T want AI-generated imagery anywhere*

# AXIS 7 — DENSITY GRAMMAR

Density grammar is the system of decisions about: how much information lives on the screen at once, how it's spaced, what the relationship is between content and whitespace, and what the user's expected level of expertise is. Density is a real axis — it's a decision designers make consciously — but it overlaps with layout (which arranges) and component (which sizes individual atoms). Density grammar is the *meta-decision* about how dense the page should feel.

The Material Design spec explicitly names three density configurations: *default*, *comfortable*, *compact*. These are real industry standards. Density grammar generalizes this into a fuller axis with substyles for different user expertise levels and content types.

There are six density grammars worth distinguishing.

---

## DEN-1. Editorial-Spacious (Reading-Optimized Whitespace)

**Definition.** Maximum whitespace, generous margins, line lengths optimized for reading (32–42rem typically). The grammar of long-form publications, premium hospitality marketing, considered editorial. The page expects the user to *read*, not scan.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Marketing-Airy* — Editorial-Spacious uses whitespace to support sustained reading; Marketing-Airy uses whitespace to make claims feel premium. Versus *Document/Prose* density — Editorial-Spacious has designed whitespace; Document/Prose is browser-default.

**Substyles.**
- **Long-Form Reading.** Content optimized for sustained reading — single column, comfortable line length, generous line height (1.5–1.7), large font size (16–18px).
- **Article-Hero Reading.** Designed hero with large typography, then long-form reading body. Editorial publications, considered Substacks.
- **Magazine-Spaced.** Generous margins around editorial content, typography that breathes, large photography integrated with text.

**Canonical examples.** Aman site copy, Apartamento article pages, Cereal Magazine, NYT longform, Stripe Press, considered editorial publications.

**Internal logic.**
- Body text 16–18px minimum (sometimes 20+ for premium feel).
- Line height 1.5–1.7 — generous.
- Line length 32–42rem (50–75 characters per line for reading optimization).
- Section margins 80–160px on desktop.
- The page is *quiet* — few elements, much whitespace, reading rhythm dominates.

**Registers it hosts.** Editorial publishing, premium hospitality, considered brand sites, long-form journalism, premium documentation.

**Registers it resists.** SaaS marketing, application UIs, dashboards, e-commerce, anything scannable-first.

**Failure mode.** AI applies generic Tailwind spacing (smaller margins, tighter line-height) to editorial content, breaking the reading experience. Or it applies Editorial-Spacious to marketing content where Marketing-Airy would have been correct, making the page feel slow.

- **Vibes:** *spacious* — *airy reading* — *generous whitespace* — *like a magazine* — *room to breathe* — *unhurried* — *long-form layout*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Aman* — *like Apartamento articles* — *like Stripe Press* — *like NYT longform*
- **Vernacular labels:** *spacious layout* — *editorial spacing* — *generous whitespace* — *reading-optimized* — *long-form layout*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not cramped* — *not dashboard-dense* — *not feature-grid* — *not scannable summaries*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want the page to support sustained reading* — *make it feel premium through whitespace* — *the user should feel they have time*

---

## DEN-2. Marketing-Airy (Premium-Spacious)

**Definition.** Generous spacing on marketing pages, but oriented toward signaling premium-ness rather than supporting reading. Section margins are generous, hero areas dominant, components are spaced with deliberate breathing room. The grammar of premium product marketing.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Editorial-Spacious* — Marketing-Airy supports brand communication, not reading; Editorial-Spacious is reading-first. Versus *Standard-Marketing* — Marketing-Airy is *more* spacious than the SaaS default; Standard-Marketing is the default.

**Substyles.**
- **Apple-Spacious.** Large hero, generous internal padding in sections, careful spacing throughout. Premium consumer hardware, considered tech brands.
- **Single-Idea-Per-Screen.** Each section presents one idea with substantial whitespace around it. Premium B2B, considered consumer products.
- **Editorial-Marketing Hybrid.** Marketing pages with editorial-coded spacing — long sections, considered typography, premium feel.

**Canonical examples.** Apple product pages, Tesla product pages, considered B2B with long sales cycles, premium hardware launches, premium consumer brands.

**Internal logic.**
- Section heights are tall — often more than one screen each on desktop.
- Component padding is generous — buttons with substantial internal padding, cards with generous margins.
- One central idea per visible screen.
- Often paired with Brand-Stack layout, Functional-Snappy or Restrained-Atmospheric motion.

**Registers it hosts.** Premium consumer hardware, considered B2B, premium services, brand-led product marketing.

**Registers it resists.** Standard SaaS marketing (where Standard-Marketing density is the convention). Application UIs. Dashboards. Anything information-dense.

**Failure mode.** AI applies generic Tailwind spacing to what should have been Marketing-Airy, producing cramped premium-aspiring marketing. Or it applies Marketing-Airy to standard SaaS contexts, wasting space and reducing scanability.

- **Vibes:** *premium spacious* — *Apple-style spacing* — *generous marketing* — *one idea per screen*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like an Apple product page* — *like Tesla* — *like a premium hardware launch*
- **Vernacular labels:** *spacious marketing* — *premium spacing* — *editorial-marketing hybrid* — *generous layout*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not cramped* — *not standard SaaS* — *not feature-bullets-tight*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want it to feel premium through spacing* — *each section deserves room* — *I'm communicating, not converting*

---

## DEN-3. Standard-Marketing (The Modern SaaS Default)

**Definition.** The default modern SaaS marketing density — Tailwind-coded spacing, sections roughly screen-tall, components spaced with moderate padding, content scannable. The AI default for marketing pages.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Marketing-Airy* — Standard-Marketing uses moderate spacing; Marketing-Airy is consciously more generous. Versus *Marketing-Dense* — Standard-Marketing is the middle ground; Marketing-Dense is consciously tighter.

**Substyles.**
- **Tailwind-Default Marketing.** The exact default that v0/Lovable/Bolt produce. Section padding 24px, component padding 16px, body 16px, line-height 1.5.
- **YC-Standard Marketing.** Slightly tighter than Tailwind-default — the conversion-optimized version of standard marketing density.
- **Modern-SaaS Marketing.** Slightly more refined than Tailwind-default — Stripe, Linear marketing operate at this level.

**Canonical examples.** Most YC company landing pages, most v0/Lovable outputs, most modern SaaS marketing (Stripe, Linear, Vercel marketing pages), most B2B marketing.

**Internal logic.**
- This *is* the AI default. Identifying it as a grammar lets Palate route briefs *away* from it when register demands different density.
- Section padding 48–96px on desktop; component padding 16–24px; body 16px; line-height 1.5.
- Multiple ideas per screen, but spaced with breathing room.
- Often paired with Conversion-Stack or Marketing-Bento layout, Soft-Container components.

**Registers it hosts.** SaaS marketing, B2B, modern consumer products marketing, fintech marketing, the vast majority of commercial web traffic.

**Registers it resists.** Editorial publishing (where Editorial-Spacious is correct). Premium hardware (where Marketing-Airy is correct). Application UIs (where Application-Dense is correct).

**Failure mode.** This is the AI default. The failure mode is using it for *everything* — when it's the wrong register for hospitality, editorial, premium hardware, or application contexts.

- **Vibes:** *modern SaaS* — *Tailwind-default* — *standard landing page* — *typical startup site* — *moderate spacing*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Stripe* — *like Linear marketing* — *typical YC landing page* — *standard v0 output*
- **Vernacular labels:** *standard marketing* — *modern SaaS* — *Tailwind default* — *typical landing page density*
- **Anti-vibes:** *(this IS the default — the failure is using it where it doesn't belong, not using it well)*
- **Compositional intent:** *I want a standard, trustworthy marketing page* — *modern SaaS density* — *I'm building a typical product page*

---

## DEN-4. Marketing-Dense (Enterprise-Serious)

**Definition.** Marketing pages that are consciously denser than the SaaS default — tighter spacing, smaller typography, more on-screen, signaling that "this product handles serious data." The grammar of B2B marketing for sophisticated audiences.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Standard-Marketing* — Marketing-Dense is consciously tighter (12–20px component padding, 14–15px body); Standard-Marketing is the default. Versus *Application-Dense* — Marketing-Dense is for marketing surfaces; Application-Dense is for application UIs.

**Substyles.**
- **Bloomberg-Dense Marketing.** Information-dense marketing for financial and technical products. Bloomberg Terminal marketing, financial product marketing.
- **Developer-Tool Dense.** Dense marketing pages with code samples, documentation snippets, technical content. Linear's developer marketing, Stripe's API page.
- **Documentation-Adjacent.** Marketing pages that blend with documentation — denser than standard marketing but still hosting brand and conversion elements.

**Canonical examples.** Bloomberg Terminal marketing, financial product marketing for institutional users, Linear's developer-coded marketing pages, Stripe's API page, technical product marketing.

**Internal logic.**
- Body 14–15px, component padding 12–20px, section padding 32–64px.
- More content per screen than Standard-Marketing.
- Often features code snippets, data tables, or technical content within marketing surfaces.
- Often paired with Marketing-Density components, Mono or Geometric-Modernist typography.

**Registers it hosts.** B2B marketing for sophisticated audiences, financial product marketing, developer tool marketing, technical product marketing.

**Registers it resists.** Consumer products. Hospitality. Editorial. Anywhere warmth or accessibility matters more than density signaling seriousness.

**Failure mode.** AI conflates Marketing-Dense with Application-Dense, producing marketing pages that feel like dashboards. Or conflates with Standard-Marketing, producing overly-spaced marketing that fails to signal "serious tool."

- **Vibes:** *enterprise serious* — *technical marketing* — *dense but communicative* — *Bloomberg-coded* — *developer-tool marketing*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Bloomberg Terminal marketing* — *like Stripe's API page* — *like Linear's developer marketing*
- **Vernacular labels:** *enterprise marketing density* — *technical marketing* — *dense marketing* — *developer-marketing density*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not consumer-friendly* — *not warm* — *not airy* — *not standard SaaS density*
- **Compositional intent:** *I'm marketing to sophisticated users* — *density signals seriousness* — *the marketing surface should match the product's complexity*

---

## DEN-5. Application-Dense (Power-User UI)

**Definition.** True application UI density — dashboards, IDEs, data tables, admin panels. Tight padding (4–12px), small typography (12–14px), minimal spacing, optimized for users who *live in* the interface and need maximum information per screen.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Marketing-Dense* — Application-Dense is for application UIs; Marketing-Dense is for marketing surfaces. Versus *Hyper-Dense* — Application-Dense uses standard application density (Material's "compact" mode); Hyper-Dense pushes further into Bloomberg-Terminal territory.

**Substyles.**
- **Material-Compact.** Material Design's "compact" density configuration applied — tight but coherent. Modern B2B applications.
- **Linear-Coded Density.** Linear's specific application density — tight but with deliberate hierarchy. Modern application UIs targeting power users.
- **GitHub-Coded Density.** GitHub-style density for developer tools — code-dense but readable.
- **Dashboard-Compact.** Dense KPI dashboards with maximum metrics per screen. Analytics products, executive dashboards.

**Canonical examples.** Linear app, Stripe Dashboard, GitHub, Notion app, Asana, every modern B2B application UI, Material Design "compact" examples.

**Internal logic.**
- Body 12–14px, component padding 4–12px, row heights 32–40px.
- Hover states are immediate, multiple actions per row, sortable columns, filterable lists.
- Information density is the *point* — generous spacing wastes the user's screen real estate.
- Often paired with SPA / Dashboard layout, Application-Density components, Functional-Snappy motion, Application Single-Accent color.

**Registers it hosts.** B2B SaaS application UIs, dashboards, productivity tools, financial applications, healthcare applications, admin panels, dev tools.

**Registers it resists.** Marketing pages (where Marketing-Density's looser version is correct). Anywhere users are first-time visitors. Anywhere narrative or warmth matters.

**Failure mode.** AI applies marketing-grammar density (24–32px component padding, 16px body) to dashboard interfaces, wasting screen space and reducing information density. Or applies Application-Dense to marketing pages, producing tool-UI-coded marketing that feels cold.

- **Vibes:** *dashboard density* — *power-user UI* — *information-dense* — *workspace density* — *for daily use*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Linear app* — *like Stripe Dashboard* — *like GitHub* — *like Notion app*
- **Vernacular labels:** *application density* — *dashboard density* — *compact mode* — *Material compact* — *power-user density*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not for marketing* — *not airy* — *not for first-time users* — *not warm*
- **Compositional intent:** *the user lives in this interface* — *every pixel must serve information density* — *I'm building for power users*

---

## DEN-6. Hyper-Dense (Information-Maximalist)

**Definition.** Density beyond standard application UIs — Bloomberg Terminal territory, where every pixel is intentional and the interface presents enormous information density to expert users. Rare but important to name because briefs explicitly route to it.

**Distinguishing edge.** Versus *Application-Dense* — Hyper-Dense is consciously denser than Material's "compact" — it's denser than typical modern dashboards. Distinct grammar because the rules differ (smaller type, tighter spacing, often grid-coded).

**Substyles.**
- **Bloomberg-Style Hyper-Dense.** The reference for extreme information density that works — multi-panel layouts with enormous on-screen data. Financial trading interfaces.
- **Spreadsheet-Hyper-Dense.** Spreadsheet-coded density — tens or hundreds of cells visible, with row heights as small as 20–24px. Productivity tools, planning software, analytical products.
- **IDE-Hyper-Dense.** IDE-coded density — code editors with multiple panels, terminals, file trees, all simultaneously visible. Developer tools, code-first products.

**Canonical examples.** Bloomberg Terminal, professional trading platforms, advanced spreadsheet products, multi-pane IDEs (VS Code with multiple panels open), advanced analytics products targeting power users.

**Internal logic.**
- Body 11–13px, row heights 20–28px, component padding 2–8px.
- Multi-pane layouts with several distinct dense areas visible simultaneously.
- The user is an *expert* — explicit affordances are reduced because the user knows the interface.
- Custom keyboard shortcuts, expert-level interaction patterns, no hand-holding.
- Often paired with Mono typography, Dark-Mode color, Application-Density components.

**Registers it hosts.** Financial trading, advanced analytics, expert productivity tools, advanced developer tools, professional creative tools (Premiere, DaVinci Resolve, Logic Pro).

**Registers it resists.** Anything for first-time users. Anything consumer. Marketing pages. Anywhere accessibility (in the broadest sense — including readability) matters more than expert efficiency.

**Failure mode.** AI rarely produces this grammar without explicit instruction — it requires deliberate density choices that the model defaults won't generate. When forced, the result often lacks the *organizational discipline* that makes real Hyper-Dense interfaces usable; without that, hyper-density just becomes chaos.

- **Vibes:** *Bloomberg Terminal density* — *trading platform* — *expert UI* — *power-power-user UI* — *information maximalist*
- **Brand exemplars:** *like Bloomberg Terminal* — *like a trading platform* — *like a multi-pane IDE* — *like advanced analytics tools*
- **Vernacular labels:** *hyper-dense* — *Bloomberg-style* — *trading-platform density* — *expert UI density* — *information maximalist*
- **Anti-vibes:** *not for casual users* — *not for first-time users* — *not consumer* — *not marketing*
- **Compositional intent:** *the user is an expert who lives here* — *I need maximum information per screen* — *I'm building for professionals who don't need hand-holding*

# How these axes interact (preview)

Imagery and density are tightly coupled with several other axes — particularly layout and component grammar. Some natural co-occurrences:

- **Editorial Photography + Editorial-Spacious + Vertical-Rhythm Editorial layout** = the luxury hospitality vocabulary
- **Lifestyle Photography + Marketing-Airy + Brand-Stack layout** = premium consumer brand vocabulary
- **3D Render + Standard-Marketing + Marketing-Bento layout** = modern AI startup vocabulary
- **Custom Illustration + Standard-Marketing + Conversion-Stack layout** = friendly fintech / consumer SaaS vocabulary
- **Stock Photography + Standard-Marketing + Conversion-Stack layout** = the AI-default failure mode
- **Application-Dense + Mono-Density components + SPA / Dashboard layout** = modern application UI vocabulary
- **Hyper-Dense + Application-Density components + Mono typography + Dark-Mode color** = professional power-user tool vocabulary

Some combinations are *broken*:

- **Editorial Photography + Application-Dense** = photography demands space; application density refuses it
- **Stock Photography + Editorial-Spacious** = the spacing reads as premium but the imagery undermines the register
- **AI-Generated Imagery + Editorial Photography register** = a luxury hospitality site with AI-generated heroes is broken trust
- **Hyper-Dense + Lifestyle Photography** = lifestyle photography wants atmosphere, hyper-density wants efficiency

Some combinations are *novel-but-coherent*:

- **Documentary Photography + Application-Dense** = a journalism-coded application UI (rare but defensible for newsroom CMS or journalist-facing tools)
- **Custom Illustration + Hyper-Dense** = expert tools with branded illustrated empty states and onboarding (Linear's empty states approach this register)
- **3D Render + Editorial-Spacious** = a premium product page with abstract 3D hero in editorial spacing (Apple does this)

The full Compatibility Model in Turn 8 will formalize this matrix.

Currently splitting them; will revisit if the Compatibility Model exposes redundancy.
