# Aman Tokyo

## Why this works

Aman Tokyo's site is the clearest expression of a principle most luxury brands talk about and few execute: that *withholding* is the message. The site refuses to sell. There is no urgency, no social proof, no "from $X per night," no carousel of awards. It assumes you already know who Aman is, and if you don't, you're not the customer. This confidence is the entire design language. Everything visual — the vertical rhythm, the unhurried scroll, the breathing room around every photograph, the serif type that never raises its voice — is in service of that one editorial decision.

The second thing it gets right is materiality through photography. The site is mostly images, but the images are not "marketing photography." They are architectural, human-absent or human-distant, attentive to surface and light. Stone, paper, wood grain, water, shadow. You feel the building before you read about it. This is the inverse of how 99% of hotel websites work, which lead with smiling people in robes.

## Visual DNA

**Palette**
- `surface-paper`: off-white, warm, very slight cream undertone — never pure #FFFFFF
- `ink`: deep charcoal, almost-black but not — sits around #1A1A1A to #222
- `stone`: a single neutral mid-tone for secondary surfaces, drawn from the photography itself
- No accent color. No brand "purple" or "teal." The photography supplies all chromatic interest.
- Net palette: 3 colors. That's it. The discipline is the point.

**Typography**
- Display: a transitional or modern serif with restrained character — think along the lines of GT Sectra, Canela, or Tiempos Headline. Never Playfair (too wedding-industrial), never Cormorant (too thin and decorative).
- Body: same serif family for body text, or a quiet humanist sans like Söhne or Untitled Sans. No Inter, no system-ui.
- Scale: dramatic. Display is genuinely large (96px+ on desktop), body is genuinely small and well-leaded (15-16px / 1.7 line-height). The contrast between scales is the rhythm.
- Letter-spacing: tight on display, neutral on body. Never the loose tracked-out caps that signal "luxury" to people who don't understand luxury.

**Layout archetype**
- *Vertical-rhythm editorial.* Single column. One idea per screen. The page is read like a printed essay, not scanned like a brochure.
- Asymmetric placement of images and text — never centered slabs of marketing copy.
- Generous, almost wasteful negative space. Empty space is treated as a material.
- Full-bleed photography alternating with deeply-margined text passages. The contrast in width creates rhythm.

**Motion language**
- Almost none. Slight fade-in on scroll, maybe a parallax on hero photography, nothing more.
- *Stillness as luxury.* Movement implies effort to capture attention. Aman doesn't compete for attention.
- No scroll-triggered text reveals, no number-counting stats, no marquee tickers, no animated SVGs.

**Photography style**
- Natural light. Architectural framing. Compositions are quiet — a single object, a corner of a room, a view through a window.
- Humans are absent, distant, or out-of-focus. When present, they are guests in the space, not models presenting it.
- No drone shots of the property exterior with the brand logo overlaid.
- Color-graded toward warm neutrals; deep blacks preserved; never the orange-and-teal of stock travel photography.

**Information density**
- Very low. A landing page might have 6-8 short paragraphs of body copy total.
- The site assumes the reader will spend time. It does not optimize for skimming.

**Interaction surface**
- Almost no buttons. Links are inline, underlined, in body type — they read like footnotes, not CTAs.
- The "Reserve" function is present but never the visual focus. It lives in the navigation, quiet.
- No floating elements. No sticky CTAs. No chat bubbles. No cookie banner that takes a third of the screen.

## What this is NOT

Loading any of these breaks the language entirely:

- A "Book Your Stay" button that pulses, glows, or contrasts hard against the page
- Star ratings, review counts, or "Voted #1" badges anywhere on the page
- "From $1,200 / night" pricing chips on the room cards
- A three-column "Amenities" grid with Lucide icons (spa, pool, restaurant, gym...)
- Carousel hero with five rotating images and overlay text
- Any gradient. Any.
- Rounded corners above 4px on anything
- Drop shadows on cards
- Trust signals: "Secure Booking," "Best Price Guarantee," partner logos
- Newsletter popups, exit-intent modals, "Subscribe for 10% off"
- Emoji anywhere
- Sans-serif display type
- Pure white backgrounds
- Centered hero text with a CTA below it

## Transferable patterns

1. Three-color discipline. Pick a paper, an ink, and a stone. Refuse a fourth.
2. Serif body type at small size with generous leading.
3. Single-column vertical rhythm with full-bleed photography breaks.
4. Inline links instead of buttons.
5. Photography that avoids people performing happiness. Architectural, atmospheric, restrained.
6. Letting white space do the work.

## When NOT to use this archetype

- Anything aimed at younger / first-time luxury customers
- Lifestyle / personality-driven hospitality
- F&B with energy and theatre
- Properties that depend on amenity-led decision-making

Use this archetype when the property is the destination, the guest already knows the brand or the category, and the visual job is to confirm a feeling, not create it.

# Soho House

## Why this works

Soho House operates in the opposite philosophical register from Aman, which is exactly why it belongs in the same library. Where Aman withholds, Soho House confides. The site reads like a magazine that happens to also be a membership product — type-led, narrative-driven, with a deliberate texture of human warmth. It is not minimalist; it is *edited*. There is a difference.

The thing it gets right is *voice on the page*. Most hospitality sites have no voice — they have features and benefits. Soho House writes copy that sounds like a specific person wrote it, often slightly funny, occasionally self-deprecating, never corporate. The visual language exists to support that voice: editorial photography of members and spaces (people *belonging*, not people *performing*), generous typography that lets the writing breathe, and a layout grammar borrowed from print magazines rather than SaaS landing pages.

The second thing it gets right is *deliberate imperfection*. Photographs have grain. Edges aren't always crisp. The grid breaks. This is calibrated, not accidental — it signals "this is a real place with real people," in contrast to the over-polished sterility of corporate hospitality.

## Visual DNA

**Palette**
- `cream`: warm off-white background, more saturated than Aman's paper — closer to a printed magazine page than a gallery wall
- `ink`: deep brown-black, never pure black — gives a vintage-print feel
- `signal`: a single warm accent color used sparingly — terracotta, ochre, or oxblood. Never blue, never green, never "tech."
- Net palette: 3 colors + a permitted accent. Slightly more permissive than Aman, but still disciplined.

**Typography**
- Display: a transitional serif with character — Domaine Display, Tiempos Headline, or a custom cut. Italics used liberally and expressively, the way a magazine uses them.
- Body: a humanist sans like GT America, Söhne, or a workhorse serif paired with a sans for navigation. The pairing is intentional and visible.
- Scale: editorial. Display is large but not monumental (60-80px on desktop). Body is comfortable for actual reading (16-18px).
- Letter-spacing: neutral. No tracked-out caps.
- Italics, drop caps, and pull-quotes are part of the system — typographic tools that signal "magazine."

**Layout archetype**
- *Editorial multi-column with art-directed breaks.* Two-column text passages, full-bleed photography, occasional asymmetric pull-outs.
- The grid is visible but breaks deliberately — a photograph crops past the column edge, a quote sits in the margin.
- Inspired by print magazine layout (Monocle, Apartamento, Cereal) more than by web conventions.
- Section transitions are *editorial* — a new section feels like turning a page in a magazine.

**Motion language**
- Restrained but present. Subtle parallax on hero, slow fade-in on photography.
- Where Aman freezes, Soho House breathes. Movement is allowed, but always slow and deliberate.
- No scroll-jacked storytelling. No number-counting stats.

**Photography style**
- People are present and central — but they're members, not models. Candid framing, often slightly off-center.
- Spaces photographed with the warmth of editorial hospitality journalism: low golden light, lived-in furniture, books on tables.
- Color grading: warm, slightly desaturated, with preserved film-grain texture.
- Imperfection is preserved.

**Information density**
- Medium. Long passages of body copy alternate with photography and white space.

**Interaction surface**
- Buttons are typographic — a serif word with an underline, or a bordered rectangle in the accent color. Never gradient, never shadowed, never bouncy.
- Inline links common in body copy.
- Membership-gated sections use a quiet "Sign in" rather than a loud CTA.

## What this is NOT

- A "Join Now" button that pulses, glows, or sits sticky at the bottom
- A pricing table comparing membership tiers
- "As featured in" press logo strips
- Testimonial cards with star ratings
- Three-column feature grids with icons
- Carousel hero with overlay text
- Any gradient
- Geometric sans-serif display type (Inter, DM Sans, Geist, Manrope)
- Pure white backgrounds
- Photography of smiling people facing the camera
- Drop shadows on cards
- Stock photography of any kind
- Numbers-counting-up stats
- Cookie banner that takes a third of the screen
- Sticky header that follows on scroll

## Transferable patterns

1. Treat copy as a design element. Voice and tone are visible on the page.
2. Cream over white.
3. Serif + humanist sans pairing, both at editorial scales.
4. Photography of belonging, not performing.
5. Magazine-style section transitions.
6. Italics, drop caps, pull-quotes as typographic tools.

## When NOT to use this archetype

- Anything aiming for true quiet luxury (use Aman archetype)
- Heritage / vernacular properties where the geography is the brand
- Pure F&B where the food is the protagonist
- Corporate / business travel
- Properties where membership or community is NOT part of the proposition

## Anti-Pattern Registry v0.1

```
# Palate Hospitality Anti-Patterns v0.1

These are AI defaults to actively suppress when generating hospitality websites.

## Typography
- Inter, DM Sans, Geist, Manrope, or any geometric sans as display type. Use a transitional or modern serif for display.
- Tracked-out capitals. Real luxury sets type tight, not loose.
- Body type below 15px or above 18px on desktop.
- Centered body paragraphs longer than one line.

## Color
- Pure white (#FFFFFF) backgrounds. Use warm off-whites — #FAF8F4, #F5F1EA, #FAF7F2.
- Any gradient. Anywhere.
- Tech-coded accent colors: electric blue, mint green, neon purple. Hospitality accents are earth-pulled: terracotta, oxblood, ochre, deep forest, sand, ink.
- More than one accent color.

## Layout
- Three-column "Amenities" or "Features" grid with icons at the top. Use editorial copy and photography instead.
- Carousel hero with rotating images and overlay text. Pick one image. Commit.
- Centered hero text with CTA below it on a hero image with dark overlay.
- Card-based layouts for non-card content. Rooms, dining, and stories should be laid out editorially.
- Sticky CTAs, floating "Book Now" buttons, chat bubbles, exit-intent modals, newsletter pop-ups.

## Trust signals (the "non-luxury luxury" tells)
- Star ratings, review counts, "Voted #1 in X" badges. Real luxury never tells you it's luxury.
- "As featured in" press logo strips.
- "From $X / night" pricing chips on room cards.
- Trust badges: "Secure Booking," "Best Price Guarantee," partner logos.
- Number-counting-up stats.

## Components
- rounded-2xl (or any radius between 8px and 16px) on everything. Use either sharp corners (0-2px) or generously round (rounded-full pills).
- Drop shadows on cards. Use borders, dividers, or background contrast instead.
- Lucide icons next to nav items. Hospitality navs are typographic.
- Emoji anywhere.

## Imagery
- Stock photography of any kind.
- Drone shots of the property exterior with the brand logo overlaid.
- Smiling people facing the camera.

## Motion
- Scroll-triggered text reveals where each line fades in as you scroll.
- Bouncy spring animations.
- Hover effects that scale, glow, or transform aggressively.

## Voice
- "Get started in 2 minutes" / any speed claim. Hospitality sells time, not efficiency.
- "Our amazing team" / "World-class amenities" / "Unforgettable experiences." Adjective inflation. Specificity is the entire luxury voice.
- "Welcome to [Property Name]" as a hero headline. The hero should say something only this property could say.
```
